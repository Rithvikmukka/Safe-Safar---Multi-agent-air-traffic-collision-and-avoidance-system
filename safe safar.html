01The problem

Modern airspace is dense and getting denser — tens of thousands of aircraft airborne at once, all funneled through the same corridors and approach paths. Today, keeping them apart is still largely the job of a small number of human controllers watching a radar screen. That works, until it doesn't: fatigue, understaffing, and a missed call are enough to turn two aircraft's flight paths into a near-miss.

The system built here takes a different approach. Instead of one central authority tracking every aircraft, each aircraft is its own agent — it senses what's around it, predicts where conflicts will happen before they're urgent, and reroutes itself, in coordination with the other aircraft involved.

02Framing it as an agent — PEAS

Before writing any search or planning logic, it helps to state precisely what each aircraft agent is trying to do, where it operates, and what it can sense and act on.

Performance Avoid collisions, keep safe separation, minimize fuel burn, minimize distance and delay, reach the destination.
Environment A shared, dynamic airspace containing other aircraft, restricted zones, and changing conditions.
Actuators Change heading, change altitude, change speed, select or replan a route.
Sensors Own position, velocity and fuel; positions and predicted trajectories of nearby aircraft.
Cost = (CollisionRisk × LargePenalty) + DistanceCost + FuelCost + DelayCost The collision term dominates every other term — an agent will never take a shorter route that creates an unsafe situation.

03What kind of environment is this?

The nature of the environment determines which techniques even apply. This one is:

Multi-agent
Many aircraft act at once, and each one's decisions change what the others should do.
Dynamic
The airspace keeps changing while an agent is still deciding — it can't pause the world to think.
Partially observable
An aircraft senses nearby traffic, not the entire global airspace at once.
Sequential
Today's reroute shapes tomorrow's conflicts — decisions compound over time.
Stochastic
Weather and other aircraft's behavior can't be predicted with certainty.
Cooperative / mixed-motive
All agents share a safety goal, but each also wants its own fastest, cheapest route.

04How each agent thinks

Step 1 — the airspace is a graph. Waypoints are laid out across the sky as nodes; edges connect nearby waypoints. Every aircraft's flight is, underneath, a path-search problem on this graph.

Step 2 — plan the initial route with A*. A* is the natural fit: unlike breadth/depth-first search it accounts for cost, and unlike Dijkstra it doesn't waste time exploring in every direction — the heuristic keeps it aimed at the destination.

f(n) = g(n) + h(n) g(n) — actual cost from the start to node n. h(n) — straight-line estimate from n to the destination. A* always expands the node with the lowest f(n) next.

Step 3 — predict conflicts before they happen. Every tick, each agent projects its own and nearby aircraft's positions a few seconds into the future along their current paths — not just checking where everyone is right now.

IF distance(predicted_A(t), predicted_B(t)) < SafetyThreshold for any future t → conflict predicted

Steps 4–5 — resolve it. When a conflict is predicted, the system doesn't reroute everyone — it reasons about who should move.

A
Two aircraft, one conflictThe lower-priority aircraft reroutes around the conflict zone; the higher-priority one holds its course.
B
Three or more aircraft, shared zoneThe group is sorted by priority. The top one keeps its path; each other aircraft reroutes around everyone already committed above it.
C
No safe alternative existsIf every candidate route is still unsafe, the aircraft holds — slows down and loiters — and re-attempts pathfinding shortly after.
D
Priority tieBroken deterministically: the lower-numbered flight yields, so the system never stalls with two aircraft waiting on each other.
E
Cascading conflictsA reroute can create a new conflict with a third aircraft. The continuous observe–replan loop below catches this on the very next tick.

Step 6 — never stop replanning. No aircraft plans once and commits blindly. Every agent runs this loop continuously, for as long as it's airborne:

Observe→ Detect→ Reason→ Replan (A*)→ Act→ Re-observe
